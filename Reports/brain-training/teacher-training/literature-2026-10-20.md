# Copy the skills, select the colony, race everything

*Literature review, 2026-10-20, for the teacher-training discussion (`Reports/handoff/teacher-approach-handoff.md` on
`claude/brain-investigation`). Five research threads and one synthesis; the per-thread notes, with every source link and
the tags below, are in [`notes/`](notes/). The project's plan built on it is [`guidelines-2026-10-20.md`](guidelines-2026-10-20.md).
**Source caveat:** publisher and arXiv hosts were unreachable from the research session, so most papers were read at
abstract level; `[recalled]` and `[unverified]` mark claims to check before quoting a number.*

**The published evidence supports a three-part plan.** First, teach individual skills by *copying* a teacher on the ant's own rollouts. That is the only lever with repeated, measured ~10x gains, and it works here because a per-decision label carries no world-to-world noise. Second, get colony behaviours by *colony-level selection of a clonal colony in an arena that makes specialisation pay*. Teach them by copying only where a sense the ant already has stands in for the group quantity. Third, cut evaluation cost by composing several 2-5x levers: paired worlds, rank-aware racing, few-ant and short-episode fidelity rungs, and inherited fitness estimates. None of these is an order of magnitude on its own.

The stakes come from the budget. At the measured $0.65 per 100 generations of solo dig, $100 buys about **15,000 solo generations**. In a 4-ant box (1.7x) it buys about **9,000**. The published colony-evolution runs used 500-2,000 generations each, for networks of 33 weights or for rule sets, so un-levered colony training is affordable once or twice at most. The largest risk the literature names is not compute. It is a *privileged teacher whose decisions depend on things the ant cannot sense*. Copying such a teacher averages its choices into mush, and memory units make that worse, not better, when the training data come from the teacher's trajectories rather than the student's own. Most sources below were read at abstract level only (publisher hosts were blocked), so tags such as [recalled] and [unverified] mark claims to check before quoting.

## A copied label beats a fitness score because it carries no world noise

The project's central difficulty is statistical. Final brains differ by about a tenth of the per-world noise, so 47-210 worlds are needed to rank one brain reliably, and one skill step cost about 600 generations. A truncation GA extracts **one noisy scalar per world** from every simulation. A teacher that labels each decision extracts **hundreds of deterministic targets per world**. Whether the world was lucky does not change what the teacher would have done at a given state. That comparison is an inference, not a published measurement in this regime. But it explains why demonstrations give large gains wherever they have been measured.

Kickstarting matched a from-scratch agent "in almost 10x fewer steps" and **surpassed its final performance by 42%**. It used an imitation weight that was self-tuned by population-based training and annealed away, so the student could overtake its teachers ([Schmitt et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03835)). Demonstrations gave "an order of magnitude of speedup over RL" on sparse-reward block stacking, and "often" the learner beat the demonstrator ([Nair et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.10089)). DQfD started better on 41 of 42 Atari games, and the strong DQN baseline needed on average **83 million steps to catch up** ([Hester et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.03732)). DAPG is reported at roughly an order of magnitude lower sample complexity on a 24-DoF hand, from about 25 demonstrations [recalled] ([Rajeswaran et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.10087)).

The neuroevolution evidence is older and thinner, but it points the same way. Lamarckian write-back of locally learned weights sped evolution of neurocontrollers, while Baldwinian learning was "inefficient" on hard problems ([Ku & Mak 2000](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/870840/)). Lamarckian learning beats Baldwinian while learning is cheap ([Rasmussen 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.00491)). Human-assisted neuroevolution helped on some tasks and not others ([Karpov, Valsalam & Miikkulainen 2011](http://nn.cs.utexas.edu/downloads/papers/ikarpov.gecco11.pdf)). **No published study measures imitation-guided GA training of a ~700-weight recurrent controller under fitness noise this heavy.** The closest structural precedents inject gradient-trained members into an evolving population and let selection judge them: ERL [recalled] ([Khadka & Tumer 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07917)) and CEM-RL ([Pourchot & Sigaud 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.01222)).

Two integration modes follow, and they differ in what they need from the brain. **Lamarckian copy steps** run a few gradient steps of copy loss on elites or offspring, write the weights back, and select on fitness. This needs a differentiable forward pass and backpropagation through time. *(Corrected against the code: in a topology-masked network a gradient can only move links that exist, but in this genome a pruned link is a plain zero weight in a flat vector (`src/train/mod.rs` `mutate`), so a dense gradient **does** revive it -- including the target-depth link the solo-dig scorer lacks. What can undo it is the selection that follows: pruning and the per-connection cost delete a revived link unless it pays or is protected.)* **Copy agreement as a fitness term** needs no gradients. It is measured on the rollouts the GA already runs, adds almost no simulation cost, and is far less noisy per world than the outcome. Mutation still has to find the link, but selection can now see a partial improvement. Kickstarting's annealed weight λ carries over directly, as fitness = outcome − λ·copy-loss, with λ evolved or decayed. **In both modes selection stays the final judge**: a copy that lowers the outcome dies. The literature reaches this by the Q-filter [recalled] or the ADVISOR weighting, and here it comes free.

## Design the teacher around what the ant can sense

The best-documented way to fail is the *imitation gap*. When the teacher acts on information the student lacks, "this information is marginalized during imitation learning", and the result can be poor ([Weihs et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173)). The student learns the teacher's *average* action over every world consistent with what it sees. It also never learns the information-gathering behaviour that the teacher never needed: "the expert will not demonstrate the information-gathering behavior needed by the agent", and naive imitation "can fail catastrophically" ([Vuorio et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00495)). Causal-imitation theory states the limit formally. Where demonstrator and imitator senses differ, no amount of modelling rescues imitation unless a graphical criterion holds ([Zhang, Kumor & Bareinboim 2020](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/8fdd149fcaa7058caccc9c4ad5b0d89a-Abstract.html)).

The famous privileged-teacher successes do not contradict this, and the reason is instructive. Learning by Cheating reached **100% success on the original CARLA benchmark** and cut infractions by an order of magnitude ([Chen et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12294)). Blind quadrupeds trained from a privileged teacher generalised zero-shot to mud, snow and rubble ([Lee et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11251)). But in both cases the privileged input was either *perceptual* (recoverable from the student's camera) or *slowly varying and identifiable from history* (terrain, friction) [recalled]. A teacher that knows the whole map, plans exact paths and sees unopened hole geometry is in neither category. A route around an obstacle three screens away cannot be recovered from 22 local senses.

The design rule is therefore **privileged computation, not privileged information**. The hand-written teacher may run exact pathfinding and exact geometry, but only over cells the ant has sensed or could plausibly hold in memory. Training the expert "to maximize the expected reward of the imitating agent" yields "an expert policy that the agent can safely imitate" ([Warrington et al. 2021, A2D](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.15566)). Penalising the teacher for actions the student cannot reproduce does the same ([Messikommer et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09149)). A hand-written teacher can get this property by construction. Where the teacher still cannot be followed, ADVISOR's per-state down-weighting of imitation in favour of reward beat pure imitation, pure RL and their sequential combinations ([Weihs et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.12173)). TGRL balances the two the same way ([Shenfeld et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03186)). The teacher should also be **deliberately not maximal**: "it is not always the best policy to draw demonstrations from the best performing demonstrator" ([Seita et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12154)), and an early-stopped teacher can teach a small student better [recalled] ([Cho & Hariharan 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.01348)).

### The 12 memory units make on-policy data mandatory

Training data must come from the **student's own rollouts, relabelled by the teacher** (DAgger), never from teacher-driven trajectories alone. Behaviour cloning's error grows as O(T²ε) over horizon T, against linear for DAgger [recalled] ([Ross et al. 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)). The sharper result concerns memory. With a hidden context, on-policy imitation provably learns to recover, and off-policy imitation shows "latching", "a naive repetition of past actions". On continuous control, "off-policy approaches actually perform worse when given access to history" ([Swamy et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02225)). The copycat problem is the same pathology: with history inputs, "the imitator learns to cheat by predicting the expert's previous action" ([Wen et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14876)). Senses such as "carrying" or "last move" are likely candidates for that shortcut. Masking whole input objects during copying is a documented counter ([Park et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14118)).

Here the cost structure works in the project's favour. The teacher is a program, so a query costs CPU time, not a human. **Labelling every decision the student makes is free**, and DAgger's practical cost reduces to the rollouts the GA runs anyway. The source papers give no general count of DAgger rounds; 5-20 is folklore, not a verified figure. Judge the copy by **closed-loop fitness, paired on the same seeds against the un-copied parent, never by label agreement**: "causally confused agents produce low open-loop supervised loss but poor closed-loop performance" ([Pfrommer et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15980)).

### Emit a ranking over options, and check copyability before training

The brain scores discrete options, which is the textbook distillation setting. The teacher should emit a **score for every option**, such as a cost-to-go for each candidate dig cell that includes carry distance to the drop. It should not emit only the winner. Soft targets carry the ranking, the "dark knowledge" [recalled] ([Hinton et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.02531)). A KL loss against temperature-sharpened teacher scores beat argmax labels in Policy Distillation [recalled], whose students were "dramatically smaller" at expert level ([Rusu et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.06295)). Full distillation has been reached at **1.7% of teacher size** in most Atari games ([Sun & Fazli 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.12630)). Even so, every student in this literature has at least ~10^4 parameters, so a 700-link recurrent student is outside anything measured. A graded target also suits the project's "distribution, not binary" law. Copying a latent rather than an action is the RMA and Lee pattern [recalled] ([Kumar et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.04034)). Auxiliary targets such as "direction to nest" or "depth error" could train the memory units to hold what the scorer needs.

The solo-dig numbers suggest where to start. The trained brain wastes 37% of trips on cuts that pay nothing and carries loads about three times farther than needed. Both are decisions a local-geometry teacher can label exactly, from cells the ant has just sensed, so their imitation gap should be small. **That is a prediction to test, not to assume.** Before any training, log (student input history, teacher score vector) pairs from student rollouts. Fit a same-capacity network to them, and report held-out agreement per decision type, between two references: a majority-label floor and a same-senses ceiling. Add a positive control (a label that is a pure function of current senses must reach ~100%) and a negative control (labels shuffled across worlds must fall to the floor). Decision types stuck near the floor are imitation gaps. Exclude them from the copy loss or redesign the teacher. No paper packages this exact probe; it is a synthesis of the Weihs, Swamy and Warrington results.

### Self-imitation needs a bar higher than the world noise

The owner asked for a one-day head-to-head between a hand-written teacher and self-imitation. Self-Imitation Learning copies only decisions whose return beat the agent's own value estimate, (R − V)₊ [recalled], and it helped on hard-exploration Atari ([Oh et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05635)). Evolutionary Selective Imitation solved BipedalWalker by imitating an evolved set of **25 samples** with a network of a few thousand parameters ([Eliya & Herrmann 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.08403)). In this project the danger is specific. With per-world noise about ten times the difference between brains, a "successful" trip is weak evidence that its decisions were good, so naive self-imitation copies luck. The advantage threshold has to clear a noise bar measured from repeated runs of the same brain on the same world. The likely outcome is lopsided: the hand-written teacher wins on dig-target choice, where geometry is computable, and self-imitation competes only where no one can write the rule. That is a forecast, not a finding. Run the test paired, within seed.

## Colony behaviours need the right selection level, an arena that pays, and a sensable proxy

### A clonal colony has no altruism problem, only a search problem

Clonal, colony-scored colonies are the recommended corner of the design space. Waibel, Keller & Floreano crossed genetic team composition with level of selection (*IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation* 13(3), 2009). They found that the best combination "depends on the amount of cooperation required by the task" and that homogeneous teams did significantly better on cooperative foraging ([Waibel, Keller & Floreano 2009](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/992dcfff-4683-4cad-bc2e-3616d30e487f)). The PLoS Biology paper is the *2011* quantitative test of Hamilton's rule, and it is a different study. There, altruism "always evolved" when relatedness exceeded cost/benefit ([Waibel, Floreano & Keller 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1000615)). With one genome shared by every ant, relatedness is 1, so any act that benefits the colony is favoured. **Per-ant reward is unnecessary for stability.** It is still useful as variance reduction for search.

The cost of this design is credit: one number per colony. Difference evaluations attack it. D++ gave **166% better performance and 4x faster convergence** on tightly coupled tasks ([Rahmattalabi, Chung & Tumer](https://robotics.fel.cvut.cz/demur16/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/demur16-rahmattalabi-chung-tumer.pdf)). Difference rewards gave up to **10x faster learning** in multi-objective problems ([Yliniemi & Tumer 2014](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-13563-2_35)). But inside an evolutionary algorithm, where they are placed "can either provide significant benefits or destroy system performance" ([AAMAS 2015](https://ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas2015/aamas/p1635.pdf)). In a GA each counterfactual costs extra rollouts. The cheaper relative is a **decomposed colony fitness**: a sum of locally attributable contributions such as cells dug and food home. It stays colony-level selection because all ants are clones, but it carries less variance than a single end-state score.

### Division of labour appears only when the world charges for switching

The strongest result in this literature concerns the environment, not the brain. Ferrante et al. evolved clonal teams of four robots on group performance alone. **13 of 22 runs** evolved dynamic dropper/collector partitioning on a slope, where gravity cut transport cost, and **no run partitioned on flat ground** ([Ferrante et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004273)). Avida organisms evolved division of labour when task-switching costs existed, even when genetically identical ([Goldsby et al. 2012](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/08/120807132211.htm)). Evolvable-threshold models produce specialists "if switching tasks is costly", and fitness on hitting an exact worker ratio makes it *less* likely; a 3:1 split "is not easily achievable" ([Duarte, Pen, Keller & Weissing 2012](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-012-1343-2)). **If one ant can dig, carry and forage equally well with nothing lost by switching, the optimum is a generalist, and no training method will produce castes.** Arena design comes first: separate the work sites in space, make a load expensive to hand over, or impose a warm-up after a switch.

The brain already has the ingredients identical genomes need to differentiate. Feedforward networks evolved only genetically fixed specialisation. Adding **self-feedback** let specialisation evolve over a wider range of conditions ([Duarte, Scholtens & Weissing 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1002430)), which is the role the 12 recurrent units can play. Ferrante's specialists arose from "individual experience, stigmergy and stochastic switching" alone ([Ferrante et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004273)). Real clonal raider ants specialised from experience alone: workers made to succeed kept foraging a month later, and workers made to fail moved to brood care ([Ravary et al. 2007](https://antkey.myspecies.info/en/content/individual-experience-alone-can-generate-lasting-division-labor-ants)). Age moves *Camponotus* workers between three spatially defined groups ([Mersch et al. 2013](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/handle/20.500.14299/92751)).

The project's **SELF input** is a fixed random value per ant. It supplies exactly the inter-individual threshold variation that fixed-threshold models require. The **AGE input** supplies age polyethism. So castes inside one genome are architecturally available; the inputs only need an arena that rewards using them. Threshold models are a special case of neural networks, and adding stimulus-weighting variables fixed their inflexibility (Lichocki, Tarapore, Keller & Floreano 2012, published in *American Naturalist* 179(3), not PLoS ONE) ([Lichocki et al. 2012](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/182822/files/lichocki12b.pdf?version=1)). Two cautions apply. Threshold variation alone "only partially" reproduces tracked colonies; variation in efficiency and in brood demand is also needed ([Ulrich et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3001269)). And Gordon argues there is "little evidence for persistent individual specialization" in real ants ([Gordon 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-015-2045-3)). Expect dynamic, graded roles, which is also what the "outcome is a distribution" ethos asks for.

### Rooms sized by crowding need N ≥ 2 and a local stop signal

No ant measures nest volume. *Messor* groups dig on a **logistic** curve, and total volume comes out **almost proportional to worker number**. A recruitment model reproduces this and predicts that digging stops even without an explicit negative-feedback term ([Buhl et al. 2005](https://hal-univ-tlse3.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00093178v1)). Density effects at the dig face drive the change in cavity shape ([Toffin et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2773997)). A decaying pheromone in the building material controls growth and form ([Khuong et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4747701)). In narrow tunnels, fire ants dig best when some workers **idle and retreat on collision** ([Aguilar et al. 2018](https://crablab.gatech.edu/pages/publications/pdf/science-2018.pdf)).

For training, this has three consequences. First, the crowding input is always 0 with one ant, so **no solo rung can train room sizing**; the earliest useful rung has at least two ants. Second, a hand-written rule such as "dig while local crowding at the face exceeds c; leave when it falls" is copyable by construction, because its label is a function of current senses and memory. It is a Theraulaz-style threshold teacher ([Theraulaz et al. 1998](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1688885)), not a planner. Third, the acceptance test is emergent and cheap: run colonies of N and 2N with the same brain and check that excavated area roughly doubles on a saturating time course. Expect, and do not train away, unequal workload.

### A planner teacher in a crowd is copyable only through a proxy sense

For collective behaviour the owner allowed either colony-level scoring or a planner-teacher in a real crowd. The multi-robot literature shows when the second works. Decentralised controllers imitating a centralised expert reach "close to" expert performance and generalise to teams **up to 100x larger**. But they do so through explicit multi-hop communication over graph networks ([Tolstaya et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.10527); [Li et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.06095); [Li et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13219)), and "relying solely on the states of immediate neighbors is insufficient to imitate a centralized control policy" ([Chen et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.17437)). Ants have no message channel; their equivalents are stigmergy and contact, and **no imitation study using a stigmergic channel was found**.

A planner that says "this colony needs three more diggers" is therefore the imitation gap at its worst, unless some sense the ant has is a usable proxy for that group quantity. Candidates are an encounter-rate integral, crowding at the dig face, or the pile at the nest. Harvester ants regulate foraging from the **rate of contacts with returning laden foragers**, with no spatial information ([Prabhakar, Dektar & Gordon 2012](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3426560/)). That suggests a leaky integrator of "met a laden nestmate" as a memory target, or a new sense. Run the copyability probe on the planner's labels in a real crowd before writing more planner. Where the probe fails, add a local signal rather than a bigger network, and fall back to colony-level selection. The Hungarian-algorithm assignment work follows the same two-stage pattern this plan does, imitate and then fine-tune on the task objective, and that second stage is where collision-type group failures were fixed ([Muthusamy et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.19829)).

## Keep earlier skills in the selection signal, or pruning will delete them

The fetch collapse (14.8 → 5.7 food home after pruning removed a link only fetch used) is the generational form of catastrophic forgetting. The literature's answer is uniform: **an old skill survives only if it stays in what selection sees, or if its links are shielded.** A connection cost does produce modularity ([Clune, Mouret & Lipson 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.2743.pdf)), and that modularity improved retention in plastic networks ([Ellefsen, Mouret & Clune 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4383335/)). But the modules did not align with skills ([Velez & Clune 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.07241)), and a link that is fitness-neutral in the current arena is still taxed by the cost. So modularity does not rescue an untested skill.

The GA-compatible fixes, from cheapest to dearest, run as follows. **Stochastic rehearsal** evaluates each genome in the current arena plus one earlier arena drawn at random, perhaps on a short episode. A **retention gate or separate objective** treats subgoals as concurrent objectives rather than a weighted sum ([Mouret 2008](https://www.lrde.epita.fr/dload/bulletin/theses/jean-baptiste_mouret.2008.phd.pdf)). A **milestone pool** re-injects past champions ([D'Silva et al. 2005](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/18713)). **Importance-weighted exemption from pruning** is an EWC analogue [recalled] ([Kirkpatrick et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00796)): knock out each link once on the previous rung's arena and protect the links whose removal hurts it. **Freezing** mastered sub-networks is the strongest option ([Duarte, Oliveira & Christensen 2015](https://repositorio.iscte-iul.pt/bitstream/10071/9347/6/post_print_int_journal_of_intell_and_robotic_systems_2015.pdf)). The fixed floor and link protection already being built correspond to the gate and the exemption. Rehearsal is the cheap complement that also catches drift the floor misses.

Staging itself has a mixed record, and the split matters for the ladder. Raising difficulty within one skill is well supported. Incremental prey capture produced "more effective and more general behavior" than direct evolution, which settled on "mechanical" circling ([Gomez & Miikkulainen 1997](https://cs.tau.ac.il/~ruppin/ALseminar/helman.html)). Staging *across integrated skills* gave nothing: on phototaxis plus hole avoidance, "none of the incremental evolutionary strategies performs any better" ([Christensen & Dorigo 2006](https://www.springerprofessional.de/incremental-evolution-of-robot-controllers-for-a-highly-integrat/1239180)). Ferrante's case is the one that favours skills-first for colonies. With pre-built behavioural blocks, the allocation landscape had "one globally attracting optimum" and was "very smooth" ([Ferrante et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004273)). Colony behaviour that is mostly *arbitration among existing verbs under new cues* should therefore inherit solo skills well. Behaviour that needs a different internal representation may not. **No controlled study compares solo pretraining with colony-from-scratch neuroevolution**, which is the largest gap behind the plan. One cheap hedge comes from EPC: small-population winners "are not necessarily the best candidates for adapting to later stages", so carry several lineages into the colony rungs ([Long et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.10423)).

## Compose four 2-5x levers to reach an order of magnitude

Ranked by evidence and fit, the levers below are not alternatives. Each needs one measurement on this simulator before it is trusted.

| Lever | Published effect | Condition to check first |
|---|---|---|
| Copying a teacher (individual skills) | ~10x fewer steps, +42% final ([Schmitt et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.03835)); order of magnitude ([Nair et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.10089)) | Copyability probe passes per decision type |
| Paired worlds (common random numbers) | Strict variance cut while outcomes stay correlated ([Sharma](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24145v3); [PEGASUS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3878)) | Per-world score correlation ρ of two near-identical brains |
| Rank-aware racing | 29-76% lower cost than the *best* static sample count ([Martin & Collins 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09594)) | Already 48 → 22 worlds; extend to stop at the cut line |
| Fidelity rungs (few ants, short episodes) | 5-30x over Bayesian optimisation for successive halving ([Hyperband](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume18/16-558/16-558.pdf)) [far domain] | Rank correlation, low vs full fidelity, on ~50 brains |
| Train small N, deploy large N | 4 → 20 robots gave 4.4x of an ideal 5x ([Ferrante et al.](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004273)) | Density-dependent skills must span the target density |
| Fitness inheritance from parent | Few-sample EA competitive on >1,000 weights ([Morse & Stanley 2016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.09819)) | Lineage bias from lucky early scores |
| Surrogate pre-screen of mutants | "Several times fewer" evaluations ([Gaier et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.05364)) | Use only to discard, never to promote |
| GPU vectorisation | 10^2-10^4x for grid MARL ([JaxMARL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.10090)) | Needs a rewrite of a branchy per-cell sweep; profile first |

**Paired worlds are the cheapest win, and the usual failure mode is already designed out.** Agent-based simulations driven by one shared random stream lose their pairing at the first behavioural difference ([arXiv 2409.02086](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02086v2)). This simulation keys its random draws per world, ant, frame and purpose, which is the fix that paper proposes. What remains is physical divergence once brains act differently. The arithmetic is simple. Against a parent already pinned down on many worlds, a child's paired difference has 2(1−ρ) times the variance of its unpaired score, so **ρ = 0.75 halves the worlds needed and ρ = 0.9 cuts them about fivefold**. Between two freshly measured brains the factor is (1−ρ), which is twice as favourable. One measurement settles it: two near-identical brains on the same 100 worlds. Race on paired differences against the parents, not on raw scores.

**The race can go further.** Racing in direct policy search rejects clearly poor policies after a few roll-outs, because what needs to be reliable is the *ranking* ([Heidrich-Meisner & Igel 2009](https://icml.cc/imls/conferences/2009/papers/229.pdf)). A truncation GA needs only the elite set, not a full ranking. Since most mutants are clearly worse (4.9 against 6.6 full loads), most should leave after 1-4 worlds. Elite parents should carry their accumulated worlds forward rather than be re-measured from scratch, and extra worlds belong only near the cut line. AS-CMA's consistency (98% of runs converged with no tuning) suggests the gain is mostly robustness: no need to guess the right world count as it changes over a run.

**Fidelity is where colony cost is decided.** Per-evaluation cost grows with ants × steps, but the measured 1.7x for four ants against one is sublinear, so small colonies are cheap. Ferrante deliberately used four robots "to limit the computational burden" and still needed 2,000 generations × 100 teams × 3 evaluations per run, on 100-200 nodes ([Ferrante et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004273)). Swarm-bot evolution sampled group size at random between 4 and 8, and the controllers held at larger N ([Dorigo, Trianni et al. 2004](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1023/B%3AAURO.0000033973.24945.f3)). Duarte varied robot count per sample and post-evaluated each generation's best in 100 simulations ([Duarte et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0151834)). Scaling fails where physical interference grows with density ([Trianni et al. 2014](https://iridia.ulb.ac.be/~mdorigo/Published_papers/All_Dorigo_papers/TriTucAmpDor2014her.pdf)), and the best dispersal strategy itself depends on swarm size ([Hecker & Moses 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11721-015-0104-z)). That is exactly the regime of room sizing. So train density-dependent behaviour across the density range, check the elites at full N, and **measure low-to-full-fidelity rank correlation before any successive-halving scheme relies on it**.

Two diagnostics decide whether the remaining levers apply. The 600-generation skill step is either *noise-limited*, where the improvement exists but the ranker cannot see it (fix with racing and pairing), or *deceptive*, where no gradient leads to it (fix with quality-diversity or shaping). Tracing whether precursor mutants appeared and were discarded separates the two. A related observation from the literature: in Waibel's 33-weight social task, **40-44% of mutations changed performance by more than 25%**, and epistasis altered a mutation's effect in 58-59% of tests ([Waibel, Floreano & Keller 2011](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1000615)). Harsh mutant distributions like the project's 4.9-against-6.6 are normal for small evolved networks, and racing exploits them. For the later reinforcement-learning head-to-head, the reference numbers favour keeping the GA. ES needed **3-10x more data** than A3C/TRPO but about 3x less compute per frame ([Salimans et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.03864)), and a plain truncation GA beat ES, A3C and DQN on wall-clock ([Such et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1712.06567)). Neither was measured on sparse, multi-agent reward with a ~700-weight recurrent policy.

## Conclusion

The literature shifts where the effort belongs. The obvious reading of the cost problem is that simulations are too slow. The better reading is that **the GA throws away almost all the information each simulation contains**: one noisy scalar per world, when every decision could carry a label. Copying recovers that information, and its main risk, the imitation gap, is a property of teacher design that a cheap probe can detect before any training budget is spent. On the colony side the decisive variable is upstream of training altogether. Castes and division of labour will not appear in an arena where switching is free, whatever the method, so the first colony experiment should vary the world rather than the learner.

The research also leaves the plan with three falsifiable checkpoints, and their results should decide the budget split. The per-world correlation ρ of paired brains decides how much racing and pairing can deliver. The per-decision copyability score decides which skills a teacher can teach. The rank correlation between 2-4-ant short episodes and full colonies decides whether colony training is affordable at all. All three can be measured for a small fraction of the $100 budget. Each answers a question that published work cannot answer for this simulator, because nothing in the literature trains networks this small, under noise this heavy, through a stigmergic channel.
