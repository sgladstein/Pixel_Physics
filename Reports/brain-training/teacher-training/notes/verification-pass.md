## Corrections the guidelines need

1. **C2, Ferrante's mechanism.** The text says "on a slope, where carrying items down cost less than walking them".
   In the paper, an item dropped on the slope *slides down by itself*, and a robot climbs the slope at a tenth of its
   flat speed (0.015 vs 0.15 m/s). Partitioning pays because droppers never cross the slope. Suggested wording: "on a
   slope, where a dropped item slides down on its own and climbing is ten times slower than flat ground".
2. **C2/C3, "castes" from Ferrante.** In the from-scratch runs, the specialisation "did not entail fixed roles". It
   was a dynamic allocation that responded to the cache size: robots acted as droppers first, then became collectors
   as the cache filled. Only the pre-built building-block analysis gave a fixed 2 droppers + 2 collectors optimum, and
   that was an exhaustive search, not an evolutionary run. The guideline should say "a split into droppers and
   collectors, allocated dynamically" rather than imply castes. It should also note that "13 of 22" means runs where
   *most items* were retrieved in a partitioned way.
3. **C6, the 4.4x figure.** This is the mean 20-robot/4-robot performance ratio over **the 8 best controllers**
   (4.4 ± 0.14, against an ideal of 5), not over every controller. The single example shown is 457 vs 103 items.
4. **C1, Waibel, Keller & Floreano 2009.** The abstract says only that "the optimal combination depends on the amount
   of cooperation required by the task". It does not state that homogeneous teams under team-level selection win.
   Either soften the sentence ("the best combination depends on how much cooperation the task needs; for our clonal
   colony that is the homogeneous, team-selected case") or check it against the full text before relying on it. Also,
   Waibel 2011 selected on *individual inclusive fitness* with relatedness set by cloning, not on team score. "With
   relatedness 1 helping the colony is always favoured" still follows from its result (altruism evolved whenever
   r > c/b), but it should not be cited as a team-selection result.
5. **C2, Duarte, Pen, Keller & Weissing 2012.** "Division of labour evolves if switching is costly" holds only "when
   selection is strongest on the amount of work performed". When selection is strongest on how workers are spread
   over tasks, specialisation is *less* likely. Add the condition, because a decomposed colony score (C1) can shift
   which component dominates.
6. **Answer section and cost-lever table, kickstarting.** "10x fewer steps and 42% better" is the *multi-expert-teacher*
   result on DMLab-30 (one teacher per task), and the source says "almost 10x". The abstract gives no multiple for a
   single teacher. Our case is one hand-written teacher per decision type, which is closer to the multi-teacher setting
   than it first looks, but the sentence "measured at about 10x, repeatedly" should carry that scope.
7. **Cost-lever table, AS-CMA.** Replace "29-76% below the best fixed world count" with "total cost 29-76% below the
   best fixed per-candidate sampling time, and convergence 24-65% faster (four simulated landscapes)". AS-CMA spends
   more evaluation *time* on candidates that are hard to sort. That is the same idea as letting clear losers leave
   after 1-4 worlds, but it is not a world count or a cut line. The authors also report that AS-CMA was less efficient
   than Bayesian optimisation on simple landscapes.
8. **T2, Swamy et al. 2022 (venue: NeurIPS 2022).** Their latching result is proved for problems where the expert
   acts on an *unobserved context*. If T1 is obeyed and the teacher decides only from what the ant senses, Swamy's
   mechanism mostly does not apply. The general copycat failure (Wen et al. 2020) and DAgger's covariate-shift
   argument still make on-policy labelling the right default. T2 should cite Swamy for the case where the teacher
   leaks hidden state, and Wen/Ross for the rest.
9. **C4, the acceptance test (caveat, not an error).** Buhl 2005 is verified: volume is "almost proportional" to
   worker number for *Messor sancta* groups in a 2-D sand disc. Other species scale less than linearly. Halley et al.
   2005 (Argentine ants, SciSpace) report that final nest size was "not tenfold greater in 1000-worker colonies than in
   100-worker colonies". Römer & Roces 2015 (leaf-cutters, SciSpace) find that nest size "does not simply depend on the
   number of inhabiting workers". So "dug area should roughly double from N to 2N" is the *Messor* expectation, not a
   law. Treat a sub-linear result as plausible, not as a failure. (Toffin et al. 2009, *PNAS*, also surfaced and
   supports C4's crowding sentence: "A stochastic model highlights the central role of density effects in shape
   transition".)
10. **T1, a citation outside this pass (flagged, not checked).** "Vuorio et al. 2024" appears in SciSpace as
    "Deconfounded Imitation Learning", arXiv 2211.02667, dated 2022-11. If the guideline means that paper, check the
    year, or check that a 2024 version is the one meant.
