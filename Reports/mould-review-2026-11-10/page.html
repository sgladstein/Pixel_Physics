Mould Game Review

The mould is a lovely toy and a thin game. Its two settings each hold half of what you want. At 45° it does what you tell it and then stops moving. At 22.5° it never stops moving and does not need you.

What I would do, in order

  1. Make the game "keep it alive". One dish that does not end, where food runs out and turns up elsewhere. The score is how big the mould got and how long it lived. It is the one goal that still needs the player when the mould is lively.
  2. Give the player marks that stay on the dish. Lights you set down and pick up, and salt you can draw and rub out. The mould stays lively and your choices stay visible.
  3. Show the real walkers and make the mould finer. The living part of the simulation is hidden under a smooth orange gel. Draw the walkers that are already there and thin the tubes.

The rest of the page is the evidence, the full ranked lists with pros and cons, and what other games and design writing say about a game like this.

How I reviewed it

I read the code and the earlier reports. Then I played by writing down each move (time, tool, place), running the real game on it, and looking at the game's own frames every five seconds before choosing the next move. So I can judge decisions, pacing and looks. I cannot judge how the mouse feels or anything about sound.

I reviewed the mould thread's branch at commit 69df5a1, which has the real food flow and the tuning panel. I did not replay the Wanderer or the Organ.

Each claim below carries a tag: played I did it once, on one dish. measured I ran it over 8 seeds. earlier a number from the mould thread's own tests. memory something I know about another game and did not look up today.

What happened when I played

At today's default (45°), oats are a dotted line and the mould traces it

Every move I made in two full games was the same move: put the next oat 20 cells further along the line. The scripted answer to Behind the Wall is the same move, done in 11 seconds. played

So there is a small skill to learn, and a real trade between a short network and a tough one. But in both games I used oats 17 times, the lamp for 4 seconds as a test, and salt and snip never. Doing nothing scored 0 stars on the same dish. played

The mould thread has been trying to fix this by changing the goals: quick, lean, weathered, then keep-off patches. I think the fix is on the other axis. Keep the lively mould, and change what the player's tools leave behind.

Paired by seed. The untouched mould lasted longer at 22.5° on 8 of 8 seeds (sign test p = 0.008). The random hand lived at 22.5° and died at 45° on 5 seeds, the reverse on 0, level on 3 (p = 0.06). With two comparisons, only the first survives a correction.

Doing nothing loses at both settings, and the lively mould dies slower and more gracefully. The weak spot is the random hand: with up to 16 oats to scatter it lives on 6 of 8 seeds at 22.5°, so today aim does not matter enough.

Making it fun, ranked

1

Make "keep it alive" the game, on a dish that does not end

Food runs out and turns up somewhere new, a little further each time. Dry spells and storms come as weather, not as a final exam. The score is a number: how long it lived and how big it got, shown beside your best.

For
  • Doing nothing lost at both settings in my test. The other goals fall to the lively mould in the mould thread's tests.
  • Almost all of it is built: piles that run out, each piece eating its own food, starving that is slow and visible.
  • A creature that can die gives you a reason to care, which is what you said you connect to.
  • There is always a next thing to decide, so the dead middle minute goes away.
  • The outcome is an amount, not three yes-or-no stars.
Against
  • Nobody has shown it stays fun past two minutes.
  • A random hand already survives 6 of 8 at 22.5°, so oats have to get scarcer or food harder to reach before skill shows.
  • It needs a difficulty ramp, which is tuning work with many seeds.

Cheapest test: Running Dry at 22.5° with no bell, about 6 oats, and each new pile further away. Pass if doing nothing dies, a random hand dies on most seeds, and a better plan lives longer.

2

Give the player marks that stay

Today the lamp only works while held and oats vanish after 35 seconds. Only the single salt line stays. Let the player set down a few lights and leave them, draw more salt, and rub either out. You shape the dish and the mould keeps living in it. Scientists did this for real: when they grew a slime mould into a map of Tokyo's railways, they used patches of light as the mountains and sea.

For
  • You keep the lively 22.5° mould and still see what you did.
  • It gives the lamp and salt a job. I won two stars twice without either.
  • Walling a moving thing into a shape is a pleasure many games rely on.
Against
  • My one picture shows a bend, not a transformation. A single small light is weak.
  • Too many marks and it becomes a wall-drawing game with the mould as filler.
  • It needs limits (a few lights, a salt budget) and those need tuning.

Cheapest test: three lights to place and lift, three salt lines, click to rub out. Put the same dish in front of you twice, with and without.

3

Make oats a nudge, not a ruler

Oats are so strong and exact that they make every other tool optional. Three ways to soften them: give fewer; make them earned (finish a pile, get an oat); or make them smell weaker so the mould leans toward them instead of running straight there.

For
  • The mould gets to surprise you again, which is where "little bridges where there wasn't food" came from.
  • Earned oats tie the tool to how well the mould is doing.
Against
  • You enjoyed pulling it with oats. Weaken them too far and the mould seems to ignore you, which is the main complaint against Mushroom 11, the nearest game to this one. earlier
  • Only worth doing together with 2, or the player has less to do.
4

Score an amount, and remember it

The first brainstorm said this and the game still ends on three stars. "Lived 4:10, peak 1,800, your best 5:02" tells you how you did and invites another go. Stars tell you which boxes you missed.

For
  • Small to build. Fits your rule that an outcome is a spread.
Against
  • Numbers need a yardstick. Show the do-nothing result beside yours so the number means something.
5

Keep the puzzle dishes as a small side pack, at 45°

The three challenge dishes work at 45° and break at 22.5°. That is fine if they are a separate thing. Beat the Ruler is the best of them because its answer is your favourite moment, a join in empty space. Each is a one-idea puzzle, so they are a good way to teach tricks and a poor main game.

For
  • Already built and passing their bar. Ends the argument about which setting is "the" setting: each mode gets the one that suits it.
Against
  • Two settings means two sets of numbers to keep balanced.
6

Three things I would stop

  • Keep-off patches. There is no reason in the dish for the mould to stay off a circle, and the bait smell turns each patch into a trap that stops it feeding. earlier It makes a test pass without giving the player a reason.
  • The storm as a final exam. One cut at the bell means you learn the result when you can no longer act. In an endless dish the same storm is weather you recover from.
  • The "short network" star at 22.5°. The mould thread's cheating pruner could not reach it with 150 free cuts. earlier
7

Out of the dish: wait

The side-on world page found the mould becomes a single runner along the ground unless food is buried. The networks are the fun. I would get the dish game working first and take what works outside later.

Making it look good, ranked

All of these draw things the simulation already holds, so they keep your rule that nothing on screen is drawn-only.

1

Show the walkers

The mould is up to about 1,800 moving specks. The game hides them under a blurred, thresholded gel, so a still tube looks like a strip of caramel. Drawing the specks themselves gives the tubes a living grain, shows crowding at food, and does honestly what the removed fake pulse tried to do.

For
  • Cheap: a few thousand dots a frame. Real. Makes a waiting dish worth watching.
Against
  • Can look noisy. Needs a pass on dot size and brightness, and a check on a phone-sized window.
2

Make the mould finer

Tubes are about 12 cells wide on a dish 256 cells across, so a whole game fits five or six fat lines. Shortening the sensor reach from 9 to 6 on the tuning panel gives thinner tubes, more branches and more room to make shapes.

For
  • Costs no frame time. Closer to the real thing's lace. More places for a bridge.
Against
  • There is a floor: at reach 4 the mould joined only 1 of 6 piles. Every number tuned to 12-cell tubes (the length score, the lamp size) needs redoing. played
3

Give flowing food one colour ramp

With real food flow switched on, the game looks worse than with it off. Fed tube goes a flat bright yellow and hungry stretches show as grey smears that look like a drawing fault. Use one ramp from white-gold at the food, through amber, down to a dull brown, and let hunger thin and dim the tube instead of turning it grey.

For
  • Lets you turn flow on by default without losing the look. A graded colour for a graded quantity.
Against
  • Overlaps the grey / embers / dried-slime pick you still owe the mould thread. Settle them together.
4

Let the dish remember where the mould has been

A real slime mould leaves a film of slime behind and tends not to go back over it. memory A faint stain where tube used to be would turn every dish into a drawing of its own history, and in a keep-it-alive game it would become a rule too: ground already used is poorer ground.

For
  • Looks good, is real, and opens a new rule later.
Against
  • At 22.5° the mould visits most of the dish by 1:25, so the stain must fade or it becomes wallpaper.
5

Smaller things

  • The lamp should look like light. It is drawn as a blue-grey disc that reads as a lens or a bubble. A warm pool that brightens the agar would say what it is.
  • The bar overlaps below full size. At three-quarters size "Storm in" prints over the mould count. The mould thread has seen this too.
  • Sound. I could not judge it.

What design writing says about a game like this

  • Is it fun to play with, before it is fun to play? Jesse Schell calls this the lens of the toy. Your first play answered yes. Protect that, and keep goals light enough that they do not sit on top of it. memory
  • You must be able to see what you caused. Doug Church's two tools for judging a design are intention (can I make a plan?) and perceivable consequence (can I see what my action did?). The 45° mould has both and no life. The 22.5° mould has life and loses the second. That is the square diagram above. memory
  • A game is a run of interesting decisions (Sid Meier). I made nine moves in a 98-second dish, all of one kind. played
  • Not all fun is challenge. The usual list has eight kinds, among them sensation, discovery, expression and challenge. The mould is strong at the first three. Most of the work since your first play has added the fourth: stars, bars, storms, patches. A survival goal leans on looking after something, and on discovery, which suits it better. memory
  • Steering something that has its own mind works when the creature is able and readable. A written pattern for indirect control warns that unpredictable randomness weakens it. That is the case for marks that stay: the creature can be lively if the player's side of the board holds still.
  • Your own two laws. An outcome should be a spread: three stars are not, mould alive is. Every system needs a verb that delivers: the lamp at 45° delivers a dent that is gone in two seconds.

What this review does not show

  • I did not hold the mouse. Whether the lamp feels good in the hand is your call, and you already said it does.
  • Most play findings are from one dish, played twice. The Running Dry table is 8 seeds.
  • Nothing here was built. The three mock pictures are dots and a stain painted over real frames from real simulation data, to show a look, not a finished drawing.
  • I did not judge the Wanderer, the Organ, or sound.
