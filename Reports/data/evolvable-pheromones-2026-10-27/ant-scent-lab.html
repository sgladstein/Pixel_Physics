Ant Scent Lab

How the scent planes behave, and what the trained ants actually use them for.

Seven candidate molecules

Each row is one ant's walk of trail, laid once and never again, shown from frame 0 to frame 4,800. A colony round trip is about 2,200 frames. Dark means nothing; brighter means more scent (log scale, so faint tails stay visible).

On a phone, scroll the picture sideways. The two airborne rows start from one bite (240 units); an ant's own emit is at most 40, so in play they reach about 2 to 6 cells, not the 31-cell square shown for "call".

The two laws

  • Ground (today's A and B): every 12 frames a cell blends toward its 3×3 average, then loses a fixed share (the fade). Scent is conserved, so many ants' trails add up. Blending faster makes a trail vanish sooner, not reach further.
  • Air (today's alarm): a cell takes the loudest neighbour minus a falloff per cell, then fades. It spreads one cell per 12 frames and makes a square cloud.

But under training, faster fading lost

Everything above was measured on brains already trained, with no further selection. The Teacher lane then trained 32 colonies for 24 rounds with B fading at 0.25, against the same colonies at 0.03. By round 23 the fast-fade colonies brought home more food (higher in 28 of 32: 15,988 against 12,230), but their nests were less than half the size (214 against 501 cells, bigger in only 2 of 32) and more ants starved (22.5 against 8.5). So B stays at 0.03.

A re-run of 8 of those colonies with every event logged shows a different cause from the one below: store eating per forager did not change. Training under fast fading bred more foragers (72.5 against 63.5), and each one dug about half as much (2.0 cuts against 3.7).

Why faster fading helps

The food scent B is laid by every ant carrying food, and most of those ants end up at the nest. When B fades slowly it piles up there, so the whole nest smells of food all the time. When it fades fast, only the fresh trails of ants carrying food right now remain.

  • That also explains the small rise in starvation: fewer ants top up from the store.

  • From generation 45 to 124, the best brains came to rely on it. Hearing beat deaf in 20 of 32 seed runs, and beat reading it in the wrong place in 20 of 32. Deaf colonies died out in 20 of 32 runs, against 8 hearing. So what they read is tied to where the scent is.

Against the control: relying on it made colonies worse

  • The free colonies did come to depend on the scent, but as a weakness, not a tool. The starting brains already read the alarm scent, trained when it meant nothing. Once its emit weights could mutate, every mutation changed what every nestmate heard at once. Most of those changes hurt, and the brains that survived were wired to whatever their own family happened to emit.
  • The deaf colonies stayed healthy the whole way (best about 550, median about 300 through generation 185). The drift arm, with no selection, was all dead by generation 20.
  • Verdict: no shared signal formed that beats being deaf, so the gene-chosen scent palette stays parked, as you ruled.
  • A retry would need a starting brain that does not yet read the channel, each colony scored in two or more worlds, 32 or more colonies, and repeat runs: about 4 times this cost. I do not recommend it now.
26 October, the real test

A brand-new channel: signals formed, and still lost

This time the third scent lies on the ground like a trail (it reaches the food), starts as random noise that nobody uses, and each colony is scored on food alone, averaged over two worlds. Free colonies could smell it; deaf ones could not. Two full runs of 150 generations each.

  • The signals are real. Take away what the scent map says, whether by deafness, by moving it, or by flattening it, and the free colonies lose 2 to 15 times their food. At the start the same ants showed no such need.
  • The two runs invented different signals. Run 1: an "I'm empty, still searching" mark, laid by empty ants (0.65) far more than laden ones (0.12), the mirror of today's food trail. Run 2: laid by everyone everywhere, so it maps where ants are, like trail A.
  • But the deaf colonies became better foragers, storing 1.4 to 2.2 times more on 15 to 16 of 16 worlds. It is not the price of laying scent: waiving that bill moved the free colony from 224 to 229.
  • Reading: once everyone listens, every mutation to the signal changes what all nestmates hear, so selection spends itself keeping the signal working, and the colony comes to depend on it. The deaf colonies spend the same evolution on foraging. The signal is a crutch, not a tool.
  • Last test: a good forager given the channel. I started from the best deaf colony (478) and opened the new scent to evolution for 60 generations. Colonies that could smell it ended at 410, below where they started on all 16 worlds, and never came to use it. The deaf arm ended at 499.
  • Verdict: signals emerge, but on this bed they never make foraging better. My recommendation is to keep the ants' emit settings frozen and park the scent palette. What could change that is a world where one ant's find is worth more to its nestmates than their own searching (rare, brief, far-off food), or an emitter that is protected from mutation while a convention forms.
