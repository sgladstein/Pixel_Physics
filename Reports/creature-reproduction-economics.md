# Why an ant cannot afford a child, and what nature does instead

**Status:** design research, written 2026-08-29 against `1c375f7`. **No code
was built and nothing was timed** — a performance lane held the box for the
whole of this session, and this repo has measured a byte-identical binary
disagreeing 2.42x purely on machine load. Every number below is read off the
source, the assets and the existing records; §7 names the six experiments the
recommendation depends on and that somebody else has to run.

**What it is for.** S6 landed budding, heredity and per-weight mutation
(PR #126) and it has never once fired on the shipped ant. This says why —
the arithmetic, corrected in two places where the framing brief had it
understated — what real animals do about the same problem, which of the
candidate mechanisms actually close it against *this* engine's numbers, and
which of them can be a **gene with a two-sided trade-off** rather than a
constant. The last is the owner's question: *"can this be heritable and
evolvable"*.

**The short answer, and it is a fork rather than a winner.** There are two
independent gaps, not one, and no single mechanism closes both. Everything
downstream of that is the choice of which selection unit to build for first.

## 0. Findings, in the order they change a decision

1. **There are two gaps.** The brief names the birth *cost* (the grant plus
   the body stamp). There is a second one underneath it that survives even
   if the birth were free: `reproduce_threshold` must exceed the newborn's
   own endowment or doing nothing is a strategy, and the shipped ant's
   endowment (900) is already above its bank ceiling (568 measured). Fixing
   the cost alone leaves a 3.75x gap. §1.3.
2. **The ceiling is 570, not 930.** The brief takes a mouthful as worth 480.
   The shipped ant's gut is `traits: (0.0,)` — neutral — and S5's matched
   filter pays `worth * (1-|gut-class|/2)^2`, so a neutral gut draws **120**
   from a 480 leaf. The measured `richest bank 568` is that arithmetic, not
   a shortfall against it. §1.1.
3. **`food_energy == body_energy` is not universal, and the exceptions are
   the interesting ones.** `fruit` is 960, `windfall` 960, `flower` 1440 —
   two and three times `body_energy`. The invariant that closes the corpse
   pump is about **flesh** (`ant`, `chitin`, `corpse` all 480 against a
   `body_energy` of 480); plant food was never bound by it. So "food worth
   more than the flesh it builds" is not a new mechanism to design — it
   already ships, on exactly the materials a plant makes to reproduce with.
   §1.4, §3.5.
4. **Body size has no running cost today.** `idle_cost` and `move_cost` are
   flat per organism (`creature.rs:1288`, `:1329`) — nothing scales with
   `chain.len()`. E10's *"per-cell metabolic cost already prices a longer
   body, so no cost system is owed"* is **false as of this reading**: the
   only price on a body is the one-off `body_energy` stamp at birth. §5.4.
5. **Therefore body size cannot be a gene yet, in either direction.** With a
   flat running cost a longer body is free to own and (marginally) better;
   with a per-cell running cost it is strictly worse, because nothing in the
   world reliably damages an ant (E13: beetles measured bit-identical at
   `beetles=0` and `beetles=9`). A knob that ratchets to one end whichever
   way you price it is E11's warning arriving again. §5.4.
6. **The recommendation is `birth_grant` first, and it is one number.**
   Decouple what a newborn is *given* from `start_energy`. That is the only
   change that makes the anti-freeloading rule reachable, it is
   Smith–Fretwell's offspring size gene, it composes with everything else
   here, and it is worthless without E14. §4, §5.1.

## 1. The arithmetic, verified

### 1.1 What the shipped ant can actually hold

An ant banks food only while `energy < hunger_fraction * start_energy`
(`creature.rs:1673`); above that line it carries the mouthful home instead of
eating it. So the most it can ever hold is *one mouthful above the hunger
line*:

```
ceiling  =  hunger_fraction * start_energy  +  Y
```

where `Y` is what one cell yields **to this gut**, not its face value.
`diet_yield` (`creature.rs:1525`) pays

```
yield = food_value(cell) * (1 - |gut_bias - food_class| / 2)^2
```

and the shipped ant is `traits: (0.0,)` — the middle of the axis. Against a
`food_class` of ±1 the quality term is `(1 - 0.5)^2 = 0.25`. So:

| food | face value | class | shipped ant gets | a matched gut gets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| leaf, litter, moss, seed, deadleaf | 480 | −1 | **120** | 480 |
| corpse (starved), ant, chitin | 480 | +1 | **120** | 480 |
| fruit, windfall | 960 | −1 | **240** | 960 |
| flower | 1440 | −1 | **360** | 1440 |
| corpse (of a rich animal) | ≤ ~765 | +1 | ≤ ~191 | ≤ ~765 |

`ceiling = 0.5 * 900 + 120 = 570` on ordinary reachable food. The register's
measured **`richest bank 568`** is that number, and it is the check that the
model is right rather than a coincidence: the brief's 930 assumed `Y = 480`,
which is a *perfectly specialised* animal, and no shipped ant is one.

`examples/creature_probe.rs` already prints this and already got it right —
its own comment records the earlier version using `body_energy` and
"overstated the ceiling by 6x". Its `best_mouthful` term takes the best cell
in the whole material table at this gut, which is 360 off a flower, giving a
printed ceiling of 810. Its comment is careful that this **can rule out and
can never rule in**: 360 comes off a material no ant in this world has ever
touched (§7, E2).

### 1.2 What a birth costs

`birth_cost = start_energy + body_energy * body.len()` = 900 + 480×2 =
**1,860** (`creature.rs:942`), and `reproduce_at` floors the authored
threshold at `birth_cost + 1` whatever `ant.ron` says, so the real bar is
`max(2000, 1861) = 2000`.

**568 against 2,000.** Confirmed, and it is arithmetic rather than scarcity:
no amount of food moves a ceiling that a satiety gate sets.

### 1.3 The gap the brief does not name

Two conditions have to hold at once, and they pull in opposite directions.

**(a) Anti-freeloading.** §2.6 calls `reproduce_threshold > start_energy`
*"the single best idea the review produced"*: reproduction must be
unreachable without feeding, so doing nothing is an extinction rather than a
slow strategy. The invariant that actually delivers that is slightly
different, and the difference is the whole opening —

> **the threshold must exceed what a newborn is *given*,** not what the
> species constant says.

They are the same number today only because a bud is granted a full
`start_energy`. Nothing requires that.

**(b) Reachability.** `threshold ≤ ceiling = hunger_fraction * start_energy + Y`.

Put together, with `g` the newborn's endowment:

```
g  <  threshold  ≤  hunger_fraction * start_energy  +  Y
```

With `g = start_energy` (today) this needs `Y > start_energy * (1 - hunger_fraction)`
= **450**. The shipped ant's `Y` is **120**. So even with a **completely free
body — stamp zero, the child conjured out of nothing** — the shipped ant
still cannot reproduce, by a factor of 3.75.

That is a second gap, it is independent of the birth cost, and only two
things close it: cut `g`, or raise `Y`. A perfectly specialised gut on
ordinary food gives `Y = 480`, which clears 450 by **30** — so gut
specialisation *alone* opens condition (a)+(b) by a hair, and nothing else in
the current design does.

### 1.4 And the stamp, which is the other gap

The third condition is `threshold ≥ birth_cost = g + body_energy * cells`,
enforced by `reproduce_at`'s floor so that a species cannot author a suicide
pact. Combined with (b):

```
g + body_energy * cells  ≤  hunger_fraction * start_energy + Y
```

At today's values: `900 + 960 ≤ 450 + Y`, i.e. `Y ≥ 1410`. **The single
point in the entire design space that satisfies this is a perfectly
specialised herbivore eating a flower** (1,440), which clears it by 30 and is
still shut out by the authored threshold of 2,000. The brief's claim that the
stamp alone (960) exceeds the ceiling is correct and understated: it exceeds
the *specialist's* 930 ceiling by 30 and the shipped ant's 570 ceiling by 390.

**Two gaps, two different remedies, and neither remedy alone is enough.**

| gap | size, shipped ant | closed only by |
|---|---|---|
| grant, `g` vs ceiling | 900 vs 570 | cutting the newborn's endowment, or raising `Y` |
| stamp, `body_energy * cells` | 960, on top | not *buying* the child's cells at birth |

### 1.5 One correction to the pricing invariant

`ant.ron`'s comment — *"the pump closes because the value is now finite and
booked at spawn"* — is about **flesh**, and it holds: `ant`, `chitin_mid`,
`chitin_pale` and `corpse` are all `food_energy: 480` against `body_energy:
480`. Plant food was never in that constraint, and three materials already
break the equality by design: `fruit` 960, `windfall` 960, `flower` 1440.

Two side observations from the same read, neither load-bearing here:

* **`beetle.ron` breaks the flesh invariant.** `body_energy: 200` against a
  `beetle` material carrying **no `food_energy` at all** (so a beetle is not
  food, worth 0). `chitin_*` at 480 is the material the *ant* variants wear.
  Harmless today because nothing eats a beetle, and it is the shape
  `standing_meat`'s own doc warns loosens `max_standing_meat` the day
  something does.
* **Picking a windfall up destroys the seed inside it.** `Carried::into_cell`
  (`creature.rs:2914`) rebuilds a plain `Cell::new(material, shade)` with no
  organism id, so an ant that carries a windfall home and drops it has
  removed a tree's seed and left inert powder. Real myrmecochory would be one
  line the other way; noted, not proposed.

## 2. How this works in nature

Four bodies of theory bear on it directly, and each names a knob.

### 2.1 The offspring-size / number trade-off is the whole problem, stated

Smith & Fretwell's 1974 model is the canonical statement: a parent has a
fixed reproductive budget, offspring fitness rises with investment per
offspring but with **diminishing returns**, and the optimum is the investment
at which the marginal fitness per unit invested is equalised. Its three
predictions are the ones to hold this design against — there is an optimal
offspring size; parents should make as many offspring *of that size* as they
can; and **the optimum is independent of how rich the parent is**, which is
counter-intuitive and is a falsifiable prediction the engine can test (a
richer world should move the *number* of births and not the mean grant).

The engine's current model is the degenerate corner of that curve: one
offspring size, hard-coded, equal to the adult. Nothing in nature charges a
full adult at conception.

### 2.2 Offspring start small, and something else pays for the rest

Almost universally, the propagule is cheap and the growth happens afterwards
on food gathered later — by the offspring (income), by the parent
(provisioning), or by the group. Hymenoptera split cleanly on *when* that
food is delivered, and the split is the cleanest axis available for a gene:

* **Mass provisioning** — the female stocks a sealed cell with everything the
  larva will need and lays the egg on it. Most solitary bees and wasps. All
  of the investment is committed up front, from stores; the offspring is
  independent from the moment the cell is sealed.
* **Progressive provisioning** — the adult feeds the larva repeatedly through
  its development. All ants, and eusocial wasps. Cheaper per act, but the
  offspring is dependent for the whole period, so a mother that dies mid-way
  loses everything she has invested. That dependency is itself argued to be
  part of the road to eusociality (an offspring nobody can finish is
  insurance pressure to have helpers).

Both are directly representable here, and they are *not* the same mechanism:
mass provisioning is "spend a lump from a store at the moment of birth",
progressive is "the newborn is small and something keeps feeding it".

### 2.3 The founding queen converts her own body into her first brood

The sharpest analogue for a chain creature. A **claustral** ant queen seals
herself in, never forages, and raises the first workers entirely on body
reserves — histolysing her now-useless flight muscles and fat body into
larval food. In *Lasius japonicus* the oesophagus expands into a "thoracic
crop" filling the space the flight muscles vacate, and the first workers
emerge around week six. Semi-claustral queens forage instead; claustral
founding is the derived state, and the pre-adaptation for it is precisely the
ability to metabolise one's own body into offspring.

This is the one strategy that closes an arithmetic like ours *exactly*,
because it moves matter rather than buying it.

The same shape without the queen is **fission**: freshwater planarians
reproduce by architomy, tearing into a head piece and a tail piece which each
regenerate; annelid clades where fission has evolved are the ones that were
already regenerative. Fission is size-gated — a worm too small to split does
not — and the cost is paid afterwards, in regrowth. A two-cell chain that
splits into two one-cell chains is architomy with the numbers filed off.

### 2.4 Colonies do not have one individual save up

Ant colonies distribute food by **trophallaxis**: a forager's crop is the
"social stomach", filled in the field and regurgitated to nestmates, with
digestion happening separately in the midgut. The crop is not the animal's
own energy yet — which is exactly the `carrying` / `energy` distinction this
engine already has, arrived at independently.

Some species keep the store in living bodies: honeypot **repletes** gorge
until they cannot move and hang in the nest as casks, redistributing by
trophallaxis in scarcity. Others keep it in the world — harvester ants'
granaries. **Where the store lives is a real, species-level choice in nature,
and it is the axis the "store in body or store in nest" gene would sit on.**

### 2.5 When to spend it: capital, income, and how many times

* **Capital vs income breeding** (Jönsson 1997): a capital breeder builds
  reserves and then reproduces independently of what it can find that day; an
  income breeder allocates what it just ate. Real animals sit on a continuum
  and mix.
* **Semelparity vs iteroparity.** Cole's paradox — an annual gains as much
  from one extra offspring as from becoming perennial, so why is anything
  perennial? Charnov & Schaffer 1973 resolved it: with realistic differences
  between adult and juvenile survival, iteroparity pays. **Big-bang
  reproduction is favoured when adult survival to the next bout is low
  relative to juvenile survival.** That is a ratio the engine can measure and
  a genuine two-sided pressure on any "when do I breed" gene.
* **r/K is the wrong frame to import.** It was displaced by demographic
  models in which *age-specific mortality* drives life-history evolution
  (Reznick, Bryant & Bashey 2002). The useful import is not the r/K labels
  but their replacement: **who dies, and at what age, is what selects a life
  history.** Which is to say — none of the genes below is a real gene until
  something in this world kills things at a rate that depends on them.

## 3. The candidates, against this engine's numbers

Each is scored on: does the arithmetic close; what does it cost elsewhere;
what does it do to the ledger. Constraint reminders: the ledger's live
identity is an equality that catches charges that do not land, and
`creature_biomass` must stay monotone in a sealed world
(`a_sealed_colony_never_grows_its_own_biomass`).

### 3.1 Newborn starts small and grows — the brief's leading candidate

Child is born as a **1-cell chain** (a head; already a representable live
state — a two-cell ant that loses its tail is exactly that,
`creature.rs:4101`) with an endowment `g`, and grows into its body plan by
paying `body_energy` per cell out of its own bank later.

**Does it close?** `g + 480 ≤ 450 + Y`.

| gut | Y on reachable food | budget for `g` |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 (shipped) | 120 | **≤ 90** |
| −0.5 | 270 | ≤ 240 |
| −1.0 (matched) | 480 | **≤ 450** |

So it closes, and *how comfortably it closes is set by the gut gene*. At the
shipped omnivore it is a knife-edge: 90 energy is 900 idle ticks, and a
newborn has to find its first meal inside that. At a matched grazer it is
roomy. **This makes S5's diet gene and S6's reproduction gate one system:
an omnivore cannot afford children.** E5 already asks for S6's ancestor to be
"a new solitary-grazer ancestor" rather than the ant, and this is the
arithmetic reason that instruction was right.

**Ledger.** Unchanged in shape. The parent pays `g + body_energy * 1`; the
child's grant is live-to-live (books to nothing), its stamp is live-to-meat
(`stored_in_meat`), exactly as `place_creature` does now. Growth later is
live-to-meat by the same route, and `creature_biomass`'s `promised` term
follows for free because it is computed from `s.cells.len()`, which
`reindex_organism_cell` maintains on every `world.set`. **Nothing new is
owed to conservation.**

**Costs elsewhere.** `birth_cost` must become a function of the *born* size
rather than `def.body.len()`; a growth step must charge at the moment it
appends. Placement gets *easier* (one cell to fit instead of two), which
should push `births_denied_no_space` down — the counter S6 shipped to watch
exactly this. Appearance is the real cost: a one-cell creature is at the
bottom of `creature-appearance-design.md`'s findable range, so the species
looks like a scatter of dots until its members grow.

### 3.2 Fission — the parent's own body becomes the child's

The parent sheds a body cell, which *becomes* the child's, so the stamp is
**transferred rather than purchased** and the birth costs only `g`.

**Does it close?** `g ≤ 450 + Y`, i.e. `g ≤ 570` at the shipped gut — which
is condition (a)+(b) from §1.3 and nothing else. The stamp gap disappears
entirely.

**Why it is not a pump.** Total ant cells in the world is conserved by a
fission; the only route to more cells is regrowth, at `body_energy` a cell
out of a bank that has to be filled by harvesting. And it costs immediately:
`idle_cost` is per *organism*, so splitting one animal into two doubles the
standing metabolic burn. More mouths is the price of more bodies, which is
the right shape.

**Where it stops.** A one-cell animal cannot fission (there is no cell to
give that is not the head, and losing the head is death) — a natural discrete
size gate, and the same one planarians have.

**Why it is a strong idea and still not the recommendation.** It closes the
larger gap outright and is the most biologically faithful reading of the word
"budding" the engine already uses. But it leaves §1.3's grant gap untouched,
it needs the same `birth_grant` knob anyway, and it is a bigger change to
`try_bud`/`reconcile_chain` than §3.1 — the child's head is the parent's tail
cell relabelled, mid-tick, with the chain of both parties rewritten. Build it
second, on top of `birth_grant`, if §3.1's newborns turn out too fragile to
establish.

### 3.3 The colony feeds the newborn — mass provisioning from the nest store

A birth at the nest consumes stored food **cells** adjacent to the nest,
digested through the parent's gut, instead of drawing on the parent's bank.

**Does it close?** Trivially — the store is outside the body, so the satiety
ceiling never applies to it. At a neutral gut and a birth cost of 1,860 it
takes `1860 / 120 ≈ 16` delivered cells. That is a legible, ecological price:
**the colony's forage income sets its birth rate**, which is the correct
ecology and is exactly what the delivery loop was built for.

**The store is real and physical, not a counter.** A delivery is
`world.set(dx, dy, held.into_cell(world))` next to the nest
(`creature.rs` drop branch) — the pile is cells lying on the ground, and a
hungry ant standing there will already eat from it via `adjacent_food`. What
does *not* exist is any way to spend the pile on anything but one ant's
hunger.

**What it costs.** It changes the selection unit: a birth paid by the colony
is selected at colony scale, which is E8's other half (the owner asked for
both) rather than E5's. And it has a hard prerequisite nobody has measured:
**is there a standing pile at all?** `litter` decays into soil at 0.5 damp /
0.1 dry, so a plant larder may be evaporating as fast as it arrives. 1,733
*deliveries* is an event rate; the standing stock is a different number and
CLAUDE.md is explicit about which one a persistent complaint needs. §7, E3.

**Ledger.** A provisioned birth is a bite that goes somewhere else: charge
`diet_yield` through the parent's gut so routing food around the digestion
is not free energy, book it to `harvested_plant`/`harvested_corpse` exactly
as eating does, and the identity is unchanged.

### 3.4 Split what `hunger_fraction` does — the replete

`hunger_fraction` currently answers two questions with one number: *am I
hungry enough to eat this rather than carry it* and, as a side effect, *how
much can I hold*. Splitting them is **not** the recorded dead end — that was
raising the single knob past 1.0, which deletes the foraging loop because
every ant everywhere eats instead of carrying (deliveries 1,733 → 3).

The split that survives: keep the satiety gate at 0.5 **out on the route**,
and add a second, higher gate that applies **only at the nest**. An ant
standing on the colony's stores may gorge to `gorge_fraction * start_energy`.
That is a replete, exactly, and the foraging loop is untouched because the
route-side rule never changes.

**Does it close?** `ceiling = max(hunger, gorge) * start_energy + Y`. At
`gorge = 2.5` the ceiling is 2,250 + Y, over the 1,860 bar with no change to
the birth cost at all.

**And this is the same economy as §3.3 with the store in a different place** —
16 mouthfuls either way. The difference is *where the value sits between
arriving and being spent*, which is the granary-versus-replete choice from
§2.4 and is the one genuinely evolvable axis of the three. §5.3.

### 3.5 Food worth more than the flesh it builds

Already true, already shipped, and the protection it would break is narrower
than the brief assumes. The invariant that closes the corpse pump binds
**flesh**: a bitten live ant must be worth to the biter exactly what its
stamp took out of the world. Plant food never was in it, and `fruit`/
`windfall`/`flower` are already 2x and 3x `body_energy`.

**What actually rules this out as a knob is the owner, not the ledger.**
`ant.ron` records it in as many words: the 4x food scale was chosen as a
*restoration* and not raised further because *"a richer floor is the
sit-still attractor the owner's 'I don't want ants sitting in one spot eating
fallen leaves' is about"*. Raising leaf/litter/moss re-opens exactly that.

**But the objection is to a uniform floor, and fruit is not one.** A flower
at 1,440 is patchy in space and episodic in time — it ripens, it falls as a
`windfall` powder to where the ants walk, and it is gone. Reproduction riding
the fruit crop is masting, it is ecologically real, and it is already in the
engine with nothing to build. Whether any ant has ever reached one is
unmeasured and is the cheapest experiment in §7.

### 3.6 Cutting `start_energy` (E14) — what it does and does not do

E14 is authorised and it is worth doing; it does not fix breeding, and the
register says so. The arithmetic: `birth_cost − ceiling = start_energy *
(1 − hunger_fraction) + body_energy * cells − Y`. Cutting `start_energy` from
900 to 90 moves that from `1410 − Y` to `1005 − Y` — the gap narrows in
absolute terms and the ratio gets far worse (measured: bank 151 against a
1,050 bar). **The stamp term is invariant to it**, which is why a free grant
still does not open the interval.

What E14 *is* load-bearing for is everything in §5: it is what makes a
poorly-provisioned newborn die. Without it, a low `birth_grant` is strictly
better and the gene collapses to its floor on the first generation.

## 4. Recommendation

**Two changes, because there are two gaps. One of them is the same in both
forks; the fork is over the other.**

### Step 1, common to both — decouple the newborn's endowment (`birth_grant`)

A bud is granted `birth_grant * start_energy` rather than `start_energy`.
One field on `CreatureDef` with an ancestral value, one slot in
`CREATURE_TRAITS` so it is heritable, and `birth_cost` reads it.

This is the change that makes §1.3's anti-freeloading rule *reachable*: the
invariant S6 wants is `threshold > what a newborn is given`, and today those
are the same number only by accident. It is Smith–Fretwell's offspring-size
gene. It composes with every other candidate here, and none of them works
without it.

### Step 2 — and here the answer forks

**Fork 1 — the solitary altricial income breeder.** §3.1: newborn is one
cell, grows into its plan. Ancestor is a **specialised grazer** (`traits:
(-1.0,)`), which is what E5 already specifies and which §3.1's table shows is
required — an omnivore's `Y = 120` leaves a 90-point budget for the entire
endowment. Selection is on individuals. Cheapest to build, and it is the arm
the existing plan is written for.

**Fork 2 — the colonial mass provisioner.** §3.3 (birth paid from the nest
store) or its twin §3.4 (gorge at the nest, then pay from the bank).
Selection acquires a colony-scale component, which is E8's other half. The
mechanic is more interesting and more visibly *ant*: a colony's birth rate
becomes its forage rate. It has one hard prerequisite — that a standing
larder exists at all (§7, E3) — and one design decision, whether the store
lives in the pile or in bodies, which §5.3 argues should be the gene rather
than a choice.

**They are not exclusive and the ordering is not arbitrary.** Fork 1 is
smaller, is what the plan already specifies, and its ancestor is a species
file rather than a change to the shipped ant — so it can be built and
measured without touching the colony at all. Fork 2 is the better mechanic
and needs a measurement first. **Build Fork 1; leave the shipped ant's
`reproduce_threshold` at whatever it is, since it never fires either way; and
run E3 in parallel to decide whether Fork 2 is even available.**

### What not to do

* **Do not raise `hunger_fraction` as a single knob.** Recorded dead end,
  deliveries 1,733 → 3, and the split in §3.4 is the version that survives.
* **Do not raise the value of leaf/litter/moss.** Owner-rejected on ecology,
  not on conservation — §3.5.
* **Do not ship heritable body size yet.** §5.4.
* **Do not "fix" the shipped ant by tuning `reproduce_threshold` down.**
  `reproduce_at` floors it at `birth_cost + 1` = 1,861 regardless, so the
  edit does nothing at all — which would read exactly like the change having
  been made.

## 5. The genes, and whether each has a real trade-off

The bar E11 sets: a knob where more is simply better is not a gene. Each of
these is scored against it honestly, and one of the four fails.

### 5.1 `birth_grant` — how much a child is given (Smith–Fretwell)

Stored on the `−1..=1` axis every `CREATURE_TRAITS` slot uses, mapped to a
fraction of `start_energy`. Ancestral around 0.3–0.5; `trait_variance` in
line with the gut's 0.15.

| | high | low |
|---|---|---|
| the child | long grace period to find its first meal | must eat almost immediately |
| the parent | each birth costs more, so fewer of them, and it sits nearer death after each | cheap, frequent births |

**Is the trade-off real?** Only if a badly-provisioned newborn actually dies —
which is **E14**. State the dependency plainly: without starvation, low grant
is strictly better and the allele goes to the floor on the first generation.
With E14 it is a genuine convex-returns problem.

**It also carries a falsifiable prediction**, which is unusual for a knob
here and worth using as the guard: Smith–Fretwell says the optimum is
*independent of parental resources*. So enriching the world should move the
**number** of births and leave the mean `birth_grant` alone. A run in which
both move together says the model as built is not the model as described.

### 5.2 `reproduce_at` — when to spend it (Cole / Charnov–Schaffer)

The threshold as a heritable fraction of the ceiling rather than an authored
constant.

| | high | low |
|---|---|---|
| | breed seldom, always with a cushion, survive your own births — iteroparous | breed at the floor, be left at near-zero, high post-partum mortality — semelparous |

**Is the trade-off real?** Yes, and it is the one with the best theory
attached: Charnov & Schaffer say which end wins is set by the ratio of adult
to juvenile survival, and that ratio is something the engine can *measure* and
report beside the allele histogram. Again dependent on E14 — with no
starvation, breeding at the floor costs nothing.

**Watch for the degenerate corner.** `reproduce_at`'s existing floor
(`birth_cost + 1`) already stops a species authoring its own suicide; a
*gene* that drifts down to that floor produces an animal that breeds itself
to exhaustion, which is a legitimate semelparous strategy and should be
allowed to lose on its own merits rather than be clamped away.

### 5.3 `store_in_body` — granary or replete (capital vs income)

Fork 2's gene, and **the only one of the four whose trade-off is already
priced by the existing data, with no new mechanism and no dependency on
E14.**

| | store in the body (replete, high) | store in the pile (granary, low) |
|---|---|---|
| loss | dying converts the store into `food_class: +1` flesh — a **herbivore lineage cannot digest its own dead**, because a matched grazer at −1.0 draws 0 from a corpse | `litter` decays into soil at 0.5 damp / 0.1 dry; the pile rots |
| mobility | the store walks; it can leave the nest | the store stays where it was put |

That first row is the good one: it falls straight out of S5's matched filter,
which is already shipped, already heritable, and already couples to the
`gut_bias` gene. A specialised herbivore that banks in its body is
converting plant matter into a form its own kin are blind to. **A carnivore
lineage has the opposite incentive** — for it, storing in bodies is storing
in the currency it eats. Two guts, two opposite optima for the same gene, no
new code to make it true.

The one thing to check before believing it: **corpses have no `decays_into`**
in the current asset table, so meat keeps for ever while plant litter rots.
That is backwards from reality and would want revisiting the moment larders
become load-bearing — but it is stated here rather than fixed, because
changing decay is a shared-budget reallocation and belongs with whoever owns
`decay.rs`.

### 5.4 `birth_size` / body length — **not yet a gene, in either direction**

The tempting fourth knob, and it fails E11's test twice over.

`idle_cost` and `move_cost` are flat per organism (`creature.rs:1288`,
`:1329`); nothing in the metabolic path reads `chain.len()`. So the only
price on a body is the one-off stamp at birth. Once that is paid:

* **Leave the running cost flat** and a longer body is free to own and
  slightly better to have — more armour (losing a non-head cell is
  survivable, losing the head is death), more ink on screen — so the allele
  ratchets to the cap.
* **Scale the running cost per cell** and a longer body is strictly worse,
  because *nothing in the world reliably damages an ant*: E13 records
  beetles measured bit-identical at `beetles=0` and `beetles=9` over 6,000
  frames. The armour benefit is not being collected. So the allele ratchets
  to one cell.

Either way it is a ratchet, not a trade. **E10's premise that "per-cell
metabolic cost already prices a longer body, so no cost system is owed" does
not hold against the current source**, and that is a correction §2.8 and E10
both need before S8 is built. §2.8 already demands two pre-checks (the girth
blocking ratio, and birth placement as a silent size filter); this is a third
and it comes first: **name what a cell costs to run and what a cell buys, and
check both are non-zero, before writing the gene.**

Until then, ship "born at one cell, grow into the authored plan" as fixed
species behaviour rather than as a gene.

## 6. What this does to the ledger, in one place

Nothing here needs a new conservation term, which is worth stating explicitly
because "evolution is a fuzzer for your conservation laws" is the standing
warning on this line.

| mechanism | live identity | `creature_biomass` monotonicity |
|---|---|---|
| `birth_grant < 1` | unchanged — grant is live-to-live and books to nothing | unchanged |
| born at 1 cell | parent pays `g + body_energy * 1`; stamp is live-to-meat via `stored_in_meat` | unchanged |
| growth (append a cell) | charge `body_energy` from the bank to `stored_in_meat` at the moment of the append | `promised` follows automatically — it reads `s.cells.len()`, maintained by `reindex_organism_cell` on every `world.set` |
| fission | stamp is **moved**, not bought: parent's `promised` falls by one cell, child's rises by one | unchanged by construction; total cells conserved |
| provisioned birth | it is a bite: charge `diet_yield` through the parent's gut, book to `harvested_*` exactly as eating does | unchanged |

The one thing that would break it is paying a provisioned birth at a food
cell's **face value** rather than through `diet_yield` — that manufactures the
digestive loss as free energy and is the same mistake S5's first version made
in the eat verb (gating on the filter while crediting face value, caught by
`ascii` going byte-identical).

## 7. Experiments this depends on, and none of them was run

Listed in the order that changes the most if it comes back the wrong way. Do
not build anything past §4 Step 1 before E1 and E3.

**E1 — the positive control on the ceiling model. Run this first.**
Everything above rests on `ceiling = hunger_fraction * start_energy + Y`, and
this whole report is a null: *births do not happen*. A null is where a
counter hides — the model has never been shown to *permit* a birth, so it has
never been shown to be sensitive at all. `creature_probe` already takes the
four knobs in-process (`start_energy=`, `body_energy=`, `threshold=`,
`hunger=`), so a setting that the model says is reachable must produce
`births > 0`:

```
creature_probe start_energy=200 body_energy=20 threshold=241 hunger=0.9 terrain=world frames=24000
```

`birth_cost` = 240, `reproduce_at` = 241, ceiling = 180 + 120 = 300 > 241.
**If that reports `births 0`, the ceiling model is wrong and §§1–5 are
void.** (Note `hunger=0.9` is deliberately in dead-end territory — this arm
is a control, not a proposal.)

**E2 — is the concentrated food reachable?** `fruit` 960, `windfall` 960,
`flower` 1440 are the only materials that break the pricing equality and the
only ones a specialist could breed on directly. Nobody has counted how many
of them ever stand within an ant's reach. Census over a treed world: cells of
each material by height above ground, and the fraction of ant-ticks spent
adjacent to one. `ant_ablation terrain=world food=trees` is the nearest
existing harness; `predation_probe mode=preflight` is the generalised
"is there X where the animals are" census and would need only the material
swapped. **This is `ant.ron`'s own litter failure in a new costume** — a
material can be rich food that no animal ever touches, and the census that
judged S4 could not see the difference.

**E3 — does a nest larder actually stand?** Fork 2 lives or dies on it.
1,733 *deliveries* is an event rate; the number wanted is the **standing**
stock — total `food_value` of food cells within the nest patch, sampled every
1,000 frames over 24,000, on damp and dry maps separately because `litter`
decays at 0.5 damp against 0.1 dry. If the pile is flat near zero, deliveries
are feeding the soil and Fork 2 has nothing to spend.

**E4 — is the gorge gate reachable?** For §3.4: how often is there an ant
standing at the nest, below its hunger line, with an empty mandible and a
food cell adjacent? If that state is rare the replete never fires, and a
mechanism that cannot execute reads identically to one that does not work.

**E5 — the clonal drift band.** §2.6's second pre-flight, unchanged and still
owed: publish the neutral-drift distribution of lineage share *before* any
bar is set on the selected runs. At ~50 asexual individuals the null may well
be one lineage at share 1.0.

**E6 — does a one-cell creature read on screen?** `creature_look` measures
`ink` and `decoys`; `creature-appearance-design.md` already says a two-cell
ant is at the edge of findable. If a one-cell newborn is invisible, Fork 1's
population looks like nothing is happening even when it is — which is the
failure mode that has cost this project a phase before.
